# Copywriting research: HOW+HOW, Ragged Edge and Fia Townshend

November 2026. Research behind the Sorted by Us voice (`docs/brand-voice.md`) and the `site-copy` skill.

**What was read:** 12 HOW+HOW projects (case study + live site where relevant), 10 Ragged Edge projects (case study
+ live site), HOW+HOW's verbal identity, messaging and naming handbook pages, Cat How's "The Curse of Knowledge",
and three interviews/articles featuring Fia Townshend (Copy Director at Ragged Edge, now freelance).

**Attribution:** these are studio projects. Townshend's own statements are marked as hers; anything else is the
studio's. Live sites change; lines below are as read in November 2026. Two claims in the brief were checked:
the "laterz" story is real (her All Ears interview), and the Palmetto line-writing formula exists, but its steps
aren't published anywhere public.

---

## 1. The patterns

### 1. One idea, said everywhere
Every strong project hangs on a single organising idea that becomes the tagline, the naming, the section heads and
even the interface. Papier: "Invite Magic" and its customers as "Paper People". First Choice: "Proudly Picky".
Tilt: "Potential counts". Wild Thingz: "Punk, not junk". Solflare: "Stronghold of the Free". Bristol Dockyards:
"Built different". Wise: "The World's Money". Eager: "tell the truth". The idea is a decision, not a mood.

### 2. Define the voice against the category's tired language
Most voices are written *against* something specific:
- Eager against juice hype ("Juice is full of crap. Overclaims, overpromises and outright fibs").
- Granola against efficiency-obsessed AI ("Less SaaS, more EQ").
- Palmetto against eco guilt ("No longer asking people to stop. Just swap.").
- Solflare against finance that's "soft, fluffy and banal".
- Tilt against friendly fintech. Townshend: "Tilt customers don't need another friend. They need help kicking down doors."
- Wild Thingz against "better-for-you blandness". Big Cartel against Shopify polish.

### 3. Abundance, not sacrifice: name what you gain
Palmetto reframed clean energy "from sacrifice to abundance" and wrote "about all the energy indulgences you could
admit". Papier reframed a notebook as "a promise to yourself". First Choice made being picky a positive. Tilt
credits people "for what they're capable of" rather than judging their past. The job becomes the life it gives you.

### 4. Write like they write
Townshend fought for "laterz" with a z on Go.Compare: "we wanted people to recognise themselves in this brand. So
we had to write like they write. There's always a reason." First Choice speaks its audience's British register
("all the tea", "fave", "Fancy getting away soon?"). Big Cartel says "online store" and "shop", never "e-commerce".
Cat How's version: the gap between customers' words and yours "is where your brand work starts".

### 5. Local, not a costume
Bristol Dockyards' voice "doesn't imitate Bristol or fleece off its accent": it's rooted in the city's attitude, not
its dialect. The Aruba Conservation Foundation greets in Papiamento ("Bon bini, welcome") and names local species
("If the cascabel could speak, what would it say?"). Rootedness comes from real places, moments and words people
actually use, not from imitation.

### 6. Two registers
Meteoric: epic, film-referencing headlines; secondary copy "precise, technical and calculated". Thirdway: the voice
is a curator: "You don't notice a curator, until you're meant to"; it "never steals the attention". Wise: "the
most widely understood words… with confident restraint". Personality lives in a few lines; prices, process and
policy are plain and exact.

### 7. Short parallel lines, used sparingly
"Six teams. One standard." (Thirdway) · "Fair access. Full stop." (Tilt) · "More doing. Less orchestrating." (Jupi)
· "We ask. We invent." (Omlet) · "Your money, not ours" (Wise) · "Hustle without the hassle" (Big Cartel). Two beats,
full stops, one idea. They work because they're rare, and they're a known AI tell when every heading does it.

### 8. Proof over adjectives
Wise states fees as numbers, names its regulator and footnotes how comparisons are made. Tilt: "Up to $500
instantly", with "Not everyone will qualify" in the footnote. Wild Thingz: "half the sugar", "never gelatine".
Granola: free, with the limit stated ("older than 30 days"). Omlet: "2 year warranty", "Free returns for 180-days".
HOW+HOW's messaging house requires a proof point for every point of difference.

### 9. Price and process, plainly and near the action
Big Cartel leads with "$0" and "No hidden fees". Wise puts a fee calculator in the hero. Palmetto opens with "How
much was your last energy bill?" and "Let's cut it". Process is told in a few short steps (Big Cartel's four,
Granola's before/in/after, Jupi's Spot, Prioritize, Route, Momentum), each a heading plus one line.

### 10. Name the worry out loud
Solflare: "Crypto has a trust problem." Trellis: "As trust in the system crumbles, we're putting the pieces back
together." Tilt's footer: "not a bank". Bristol: "Rain or shine, big budget or small". Naming the doubt plainly
and answering it beats reassurance adjectives. Townshend: "People need to feel safe with you because they're
trusting you to make a change."

### 11. Wit with a job
Eager's product subtitles ("A blind taste test we're fairly certain you'd ace"), Omlet's "Because no one likes a
muddy run. Especially your hens.", Wild Thingz's bug-named flavours, Bristol's Brunel as "a master of mistakes…
famously impressive sideburns". Humour sits in small places (product lines, microcopy, asides) and always serves
the point. Palmetto's guidelines had a formula "to make them funny without taking it too far, to keep them joyful
not wasteful".

### 12. Questions as headings
Omlet ("Can a chicken be a pet?"), Bristol ("How many museums can claim they've travelled the world?"), First
Choice ("Where's hot this winter?"), Palmetto ("What can we help you with today?"). A question puts the reader's
situation first.

### 13. The reader as the hero
Sauce Labs "made software teams the hero of the story". Big Cartel: "Your dreams are officially in motion". Tilt: "We
see you and know you're more than a credit score." The brand is the helper; the reader's life is the subject.

### 14. Messaging house before copy
HOW+HOW's framework: tagline (the most repeated line) → one-line descriptor → elevator pitch → about three points of
difference → proof for each. Develop it from positioning, per audience, then "test the language against competitor
brands". Verbal and visual identity get equal weight; "a strong visual identity with weak copy is a common gap".

### 15. Escape the curse of knowledge, and comfort
Cat How: founders hear "the whole song"; customers "just hear tapping". Test copy with people outside the business,
"even your gran". Townshend: "Comfortable isn't always correct", and she worries when clients say "This sounds like
us." She distrusts the feeling a name is good, because "what I might be feeling is familiarity".

### What they almost never do
Exclamation marks in brand copy (they appear in testimonials and a few promos). Vague superlatives ("best",
"cheapest"). Jargon (Townshend: "We're so bored of industry jargon"). Mimicked dialect. Generic welcome heroes.

## 2. Where Sorted by Us already does this

| Pattern | Already on the site |
|---|---|
| Short parallel lines | "Life is busy. Let us get it sorted." · "More life, less admin" |
| Abundance, not sacrifice | "More life, less admin" is exactly Palmetto's move: what you gain, not what you avoid |
| One idea, said everywhere | "sorted" runs through the name, "Consider it sorted.", "See what we sort", "What can we sort?" |
| Reader as hero | "Your time, back" · "Your person at home" |
| Local, not a costume | Real places: Surulere, Tarkwa Bay, Lekki, Jos; London, Houston, Toronto |
| Price plainly | "One quote, our fee included, before anything goes ahead" |
| Microcopy | "Thanks, we've got it." · "We'll only use your details to reply." |

## 3. The gaps

1. **No proof.** The site makes promises ("same day", "within the hour", "photos so you know it's done") but shows
   no numbers, reviews or named people. Every brand above backs claims with something checkable.
2. **No stated foil.** The voice isn't yet written *against* anything. The concierge category's tired language is
   luxury gatekeeping (VIP, bespoke, exclusive, lifestyle management, "solutions"); the everyday enemy is the chasing,
   queues, traffic and follow-ups that eat a Lagos week, and the worry of organising it from abroad.
3. **The diaspora's worry isn't named.** "Will it actually get done?" is their biggest doubt and the site answers it
   only in passing.
4. **Customers' own words are thin.** The request examples are good but mostly generic. Nigerians' own vocabulary
   for the moments we serve (aso-ebi, owambe, Detty December, "back home", "before I land") barely appears.
5. **No messaging house.** Headlines are strong individually, but there's no agreed tagline, descriptor or three
   points of difference, so each page reinvents the pitch.
